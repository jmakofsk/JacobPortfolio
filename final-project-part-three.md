# Changes made since Part II
Since completing Part II, I made several changes based on my user interviews. The biggest of these changes was the addition of a third chart! This was the GDP vs. Presidential Approval Rating chart, which ended up showing a nearly uninterrupted upward trend in economic output, but little correlation with the approval ratings. This addition was very helpful for my narrative. If the economy is "doing well" by traditional macro measures, why does public sentiment not reflect that? The gas and food CPI charts help answer that question. My theory is that food prices show little correlation because they are less volatile than gas prices, following a more steady upward trend over time as opposed to fluctuating wildly.

I also added a dedicated explainer slide defining both GDP and CPI before presenting all of my data, which helps a lot with clarity. My user interviews made clear that CPI in particular was confusing to general audiences. People understood the charts visually but didn't know what the numbers on the axis actually meant.

Additionally, I improved my narrative significantly. In Part II, I had visualizations but no clear takeaway. In Part III, the story moves the reader from "the economy looks fine" to a recognition that gas and groceries tell a very different story, and ends with a direct call to action that "Politicians MUST pay more attention to the everyday costs that put Americans under financial pressure."


## The audience
My final audience is politicians and their communications staff (specifically those who shape how economic success is defined and discussed publicly). This is a deliberately narrow audience, and I think that specificity makes the argument stronger. Rather than speaking broadly to everyone, this project speaks directly to the people who have the most power to change how economic policy is framed and prioritized.

I went back and forth on this. I considered making everyday American voters the audience, since the data ultimately validates what they already feel. But I landed on politicians for a more pointed reason: Americans don't need to be told that gas prices are high or that groceries are expensive. They already know. They feel it every day. The approval ratings in this data prove that voters have been signaling their frustration clearly and consistently for 25 years.

The people who aren't listening are the ones in office. Politicians who lead with GDP as their primary measure of success are speaking a language that doesn't match the lived experience of their constituents. And this data shows the political cost of that disconnect.


## Final design decisions
I kept the dual-axis format (CPI on the right axis, presidential approval on the left) because it allows readers to visually track whether the two lines move together or diverge. This format was surprisingly effective for the gas chart in particular, where the inverse relationship (gas up, approval down) is very visible across multiple administrations.

I used orange for gas CPI, light green for food CPI, dark green for GDP, and a consistent blue for the presidential approval rating across all three charts. This makes it easier to compare across charts without re-learning the legend each time.

The most deliberate narrative choice was adding the GDP chart as a foil. The GDP line climbs steadily, but approval ratings do not follow.

I also included visual indicators showing which president was in office at each point, along with major events that may have influenced the data, such as 9/11, the 2008 financial crisis, and the COVID-19 pandemic.

# Final thoughts
This project surprised me in a few meaningful ways! When I started, I expected to find that both grocery and gas prices would show a strong relationship with presidential approval (but I was wrong about groceries). That unexpected null result ended up being one of the most interesting parts of the story, because it forced me to think about why gas is more politically salient than food. My hypothesis is that food prices show little correlation because they are less volatile than gas prices, following a more steady upward trend over time as opposed to fluctuating wildly. Even so, as food prices continue to spike, I believe they will have a more significant impact as well.

If I had more time, I would have liked to have run a more formal statistical correlation analysis instead of relying on visual pattern-matching, and I would have explored whether the rate of change in gas prices (rather than the absolute CPI level) is an even better predictor of approval shifts. I also would have been interested to see how other economic indicators affect the presidential approval rating (like the price of housing, rent, cars, etc...) 

Overall, this project gave me a much more concrete way to think about the gap between macroeconomic indicators and everyday economic experience.
